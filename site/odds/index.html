What are the odds?

Pick any moment of a game: the inning, the outs, the runners, the score, the era. You get the home team's chance to win, how sure that number is, how many real games reached the same spot, and the latest of them. It is learned from 15,211,482 plate appearances of 197,645 games, 1908 to 2025.

      A century of odds: what changed, and what didn't

      Scoring has swung from 3.40 runs per team per game (1908) to 5.58 (1930), and you would expect a lead to be worth much more when runs are scarce. Mostly, it isn't. The same spot gives nearly the same odds in every era: a two-run lead or a bases-loaded jam is worth about what it was worth a hundred years ago. Where eras do differ, the gaps are a few points, and they follow the runs. A home team down a run in the bottom of the 9th came back 15.4% of the time in the dead-ball era and 20.3% in the live-ball years that followed.

      The greatest comebacks since 1908

      For every game, the lowest chance the eventual winner had before any plate appearance, by the final model. 8.1% of all games were won by a team that was below 10% at some point, 0.43% by a team below 1%, and 35 games in 118 seasons by a team below 1 in 1,000. The longest odds of all: Chicago (NL), down 8–2, top of the 9th, two outs, bases empty, on June 29, 1952. They won 9–8.

      Comebacks follow the runs

      Put two things side by side that are rarely shown together: how often a game is won from far behind, and how many runs a season scores. Across 118 seasons they rise and fall together (correlation 0.76). The live-ball 1920s and 1930s were the golden age of the comeback (10.8% of games in 1930, the top scoring season); the dead-ball years and 1968, the year of the pitcher, its lows (5.0% in 1908, 6.2% in 1968).

      Home field: about 54%, and where it lives

      The home team wins about 54% of the time, and has for 125 seasons. Single seasons wander, from 50.6% in 1917 to 58.2% in 1931, but no decade has averaged below 53.5% or above 55.5%. In 2020, with no fans in the stands, the home team won 55.7%, more than in 2019 (52.9%) or 2021 (53.9%): whatever the edge is, it is not just the crowd (a 60-game season is a small sample, and a few teams played their home games elsewhere).

      The home team's share of wins in every AL and NL season since 1901 (dots), with a running 11-season average (line). Every game in Retrosheet's game logs, ties left out.

      Where does the edge come from? In a tie game, the home team's chance at the start of the top of an inning slides from 54.1% in the 1st to 50.1% in the 6th: home teams build their edge over the game by outscoring their visitors a little. Late in the game the last at-bat takes over: in a tie in the bottom of the 9th the home team wins 66.8%.

      The runner on second

      Since 2020 every regular-season extra half-inning starts with a runner on second. It did what it was meant to do: 71% of extra-inning games now end in the 10th, against 44% before, and the longest has gone 16 innings (before: 25). It also moved the odds. A tie at the start of the 10th was a 52.3% chance for the home team; with the visitors' free runner on second it is 49.4%. A home team starting the bottom of the 10th tied, with its own runner on second, wins 84.1% (with the bases empty it was 66.5%).

      And the home team's edge in extra innings may be gone: it won 52.4% of extra-inning games from 1908 to 2019, and 50.0% of 1,125 since (90% interval 47.5% to 52.4%). One reading: batting last was worth something because the home team knew what it needed; now the visitors start with a runner and often score first, and the home team has to answer from behind.

      Four models, one honest test

      Each model was fitted on 1908-2016 and scored on the 20,056 games of 2017-2025, which it never saw. The score is the log loss: the average surprise per plate appearance, lower is better (a coin flip scores 0.693). Intervals come from resampling whole games.

      Rare spots: states seen fewer than 100 times in training (0.8% of test plate appearances). Extra innings since 2020: 0.8%.

      • The table does almost all the work. Knowing the state cuts the surprise by 33% against knowing nothing but home field. With nine thousand states and fifteen million plate appearances, the plain record is very hard to beat on average.
      • Shrinking helps where data is thin. Empirical Bayes beats the table by a hair overall (the whole interval of the gain is above zero) and by 9% on rare spots, where the table says 0% or 100% from a handful of games.
      • A formula wins when the rules change. The logistic regression, six numbers on top of a little baseball logic, is a touch worse on average but the best on rare spots, and by far the best in the extra innings since 2020: its formula knows what a runner on second is worth, while the other models had never seen the rule.
      • The trees learn the new rule fast. Given three seasons of it (fitted on 1908–2022), gradient boosting is the best in the extra innings of 2023 on, 5% better than the table. That is the final model behind the calculator, fitted on every season.

      How it was made

      Retrosheet's event files list every play of 199,897 regular-season AL and NL games from 1908 to 2025. The lab's parser replays each game, keeps the runners, the outs and the score, and writes down the state as each batter steps in. Every one of those games ends on the final score in Retrosheet's game logs (100.0%), so every state in between is right. Games scheduled for seven innings, games stopped before the ninth, and ties are left out: 197,645 games remain. The whole pipeline reruns by itself once a year, after Retrosheet's December release.
