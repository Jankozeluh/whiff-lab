Rookie Report: will he be a star?

Every rookie of 2024 and 2025, with his chance of making at least two All-Star teams in his next nine seasons, the five rookies since 1932 most like him and what became of them, and how much of his first season was luck. Tested on the rookies of 1995-2016: the model found more future stars than the back of the card or the Rookie of the Year voters.

The finding

The most important number on a rookie card is the birth date

Of all hitters who were rookies at 20, 45% went on to make at least two All-Star teams in the next nine seasons. At 24 it was 13%, at 27 5%. Age alone picks future stars better than everything else on the back of the card, and better than the Rookie of the Year voters.

Does it work? The test

The model learned from the rookies of 1932-1994 and was then scored on the rookies of 1995-2016, whose next nine seasons are all in the data: 1,472 hitters (175 became stars) and 1,723 pitchers (108). Each test year, the five rookies ranked highest by each method (110 picks in all): how many became stars?

Over all test rookies, not just the top five, the model's forecasts beat the card's and the voters' by a margin that resampling the rookies a thousand times never erased (log loss, lab note 7).

Its percentages mean what they say: of the test hitters it gave a 35%–50% chance, 49% became stars; of those it gave under 5%, 1.1%.

What didn't help

  • Taking the luck out of his rates. A rookie's strikeout, walk and home run rates and his average on balls in play are shrunk toward the league's, so 300 plate appearances can't say more than they know. It makes the luck readable for each rookie, but it predicts stars only a hair better than his raw numbers with his age.
  • His pitches. Since 1988 Retrosheet has every pitch: how often a hitter's swings missed, how often a pitcher made them miss, how often each swung or threw a ball. The card never shows these. Added to the model on the rookies of 2004-2016, they changed nothing measurable.
  • A fancier model. Gradient boosting did worse than the plain logistic regression: a few thousand rookies are too few for it.

So the edge is not hidden data. It is reading the data that every card shows the right way: the birth date first, then how much he played, his power and his strikeouts.

How to read it

  • A star means All-Star teams. They are picked by fans, players and the league, and every club gets at least one, so the label is popularity as much as value.
  • A rookie is a player in the season he first goes past 130 at-bats or 50 innings, MLB's limits. A late call-up who stays under them is a rookie the next year.
  • Players who came from Japan's, Korea's or Taiwan's top leagues arrive as grown pros, and the model's age penalty is unfair to them. They are marked.
  • What the model can't see: injuries, minor-league records, scouting reports, how hard he hits the ball. Professional projections use those; the lab only uses free, open data.
  • New classes arrive when the Lahman database publishes a season, usually early in the next year; the report then rebuilds by itself.
