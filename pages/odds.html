      A century of odds: what changed, and what didn't

      Scoring has swung from {{s.rpg_low.rpg|f2}} runs per team per game ({{s.rpg_low.year|year}}) to {{s.rpg_high.rpg|f2}} ({{s.rpg_high.year|year}}), and you would expect a lead to be worth much more when runs are scarce. Mostly, it isn't. The same spot gives nearly the same odds in every era: a two-run lead or a bases-loaded jam is worth about what it was worth a hundred years ago. Where eras do differ, the gaps are a few points, and they follow the runs. A home team down a run in the bottom of the 9th came back {{f.spots.1.eras.deadball.p|pct1}} of the time in the dead-ball era and {{f.spots.1.eras.liveball.p|pct1}} in the live-ball years that followed.

      Home field: about 54%, and where it lives

      The home team wins about 54% of the time, and has for 125 seasons. Single seasons wander, from {{seasons.1917.home_win|pct1}} in 1917 to {{seasons.1931.home_win|pct1}} in 1931, but no decade has averaged below 53.5% or above 55.5%. In 2020, with no fans in the stands, the home team won {{seasons.2020.home_win|pct1}}, more than in {{seasons.2019.year|year}} ({{seasons.2019.home_win|pct1}}) or {{seasons.2021.year|year}} ({{seasons.2021.home_win|pct1}}): whatever the edge is, it is not just the crowd (a 60-game season is a small sample, and a few teams played their home games elsewhere).

      The home team's share of wins in every AL and NL season since 1901 (dots), with a running 11-season average (line). Every game in Retrosheet's game logs, ties left out.

      Where does the edge come from? In a tie game, the home team's chance at the start of the top of an inning slides from {{tie.before.top.0|pct1}} in the 1st to {{tie.before.top.5|pct1}} in the 6th: home teams build their edge over the game by outscoring their visitors a little. Late in the game the last at-bat takes over: in a tie in the bottom of the 9th the home team wins {{tie.before.bottom.8|pct1}}.

      The runner on second

      Since 2020 every regular-season extra half-inning starts with a runner on second. It did what it was meant to do: {{x.runner.over_in_10th|pct0}} of extra-inning games now end in the 10th, against {{x.before.over_in_10th|pct0}} before, and the longest has gone {{x.runner.longest|int}} innings (before: {{x.before.longest|int}}). It also moved the odds. A tie at the start of the 10th was a {{tie.before.top.9|pct1}} chance for the home team; with the visitors' free runner on second it is {{tie.runner.top.9|pct1}}. A home team starting the bottom of the 10th tied, with its own runner on second, wins {{tie.runner.bottom.9|pct1}} (with the bases empty it was {{tie.before.bottom.9|pct1}}).

      And the home team's edge in extra innings may be gone: it won {{x.before.home_win|pct1}} of extra-inning games from 1908 to 2019, and {{x.runner.home_win|pct1}} of {{x.runner.games|int}} since (90% interval {{x.runner.home_win_lo|pct1}} to {{x.runner.home_win_hi|pct1}}). One reading: batting last was worth something because the home team knew what it needed; now the visitors start with a runner and often score first, and the home team has to answer from behind.

      Four models, one honest test

      Each model was fitted on {{h.train}} and scored on the {{h.games|int}} games of {{h.test}}, which it never saw. The score is the log loss: the average surprise per plate appearance, lower is better (a coin flip scores 0.693). Intervals come from resampling whole games.

      Rare spots: states seen fewer than 100 times in training ({{h.share.rare|pct1}} of test plate appearances). Extra innings since 2020: {{h.share.x20|pct1}}.

      • The table does almost all the work. Knowing the state cuts the surprise by {{h.table.gain_over_home|pct0}} against knowing nothing but home field. With nine thousand states and fifteen million plate appearances, the plain record is very hard to beat on average.
      • Shrinking helps where data is thin. Empirical Bayes beats the table by a hair overall (the whole interval of the gain is above zero) and by {{h.shrunk.gain.rare|pct0}} on rare spots, where the table says 0% or 100% from a handful of games.
      • A formula wins when the rules change. The logistic regression, six numbers on top of a little baseball logic, is a touch worse on average but the best on rare spots, and by far the best in the extra innings since 2020: its formula knows what a runner on second is worth, while the other models had never seen the rule.
      • The trees learn the new rule fast. Given three seasons of it (fitted on 1908–2022), gradient boosting is the best in the extra innings of 2023 on, {{r.boosted.gain|pct0}} better than the table. That is the final model behind the calculator, fitted on every season.

      How it was made

      Retrosheet's event files list every play of {{s.games_parsed|int}} regular-season AL and NL games from {{s.first_season}} to {{s.last_season}}. The lab's parser replays each game, keeps the runners, the outs and the score, and writes down the state as each batter steps in. Every one of those games ends on the final score in Retrosheet's game logs ({{s.score_match|pct1}}), so every state in between is right. Games scheduled for seven innings, games stopped before the ninth, and ties are left out: {{s.games_used|int}} games remain. The whole pipeline reruns by itself once a year, after Retrosheet's December release.
